### In plain terms, before any of the detail

**A new Australian rule starts on 10 December 2026.** If software in an organisation makes decisions about people that matter to them, the organisation's privacy policy has to say **what kinds of decisions those are** and **what kinds of personal information they use.**

**This file is the result of opening the privacy policies of 120 large Australian organisations and checking whether those words are there yet.** That is all it is. **No legal judgement, no assessment of anybody's systems** — just what each published page contained, with the link and the date so anyone can look for themselves.

⭐ **Why a technologist should care about a document-drafting rule.** Because the rule cannot be answered from a document. **To describe the kinds of decisions your software makes, somebody has to know what they are** — and in most organisations that list does not exist in any system. ⭐⭐ **This file is the evidence for that claim, and it is the reference piece behind a longer argument about recording decisions as they happen** — what was decided, on what basis, and why — so that questions like this one become a query instead of a project.

---

**What this file is.** A check of large Australian organisations' published privacy policies for the automated decision-making disclosure that commences **10 December 2026** (APP 1.7–1.9, inserted by the *Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024*).

## Why the tables score APP 1.8, and what 1.7 and 1.9 do

APP 1.7, 1.8 and 1.9 are one obligation in three parts. All three need attention. Only one of them requires words that a reader can check in a privacy policy, and that is the part this file scores.

**APP 1.7 is the trigger.** From 10 December 2026 an APP entity's privacy policy must contain the APP 1.8 information if, and only if, all three of these are true:

- the entity has arranged for a computer program to make a decision, or to do a thing that is substantially and directly related to making a decision;
- the decision could reasonably be expected to significantly affect the rights or interests of an individual;
- personal information about the individual is used in the operation of the program to make the decision or to do that thing.

"Arranged for" covers an in-house program, a program the entity buys, and a program the entity configures. A human reviewing the output does not, by itself, take the decision outside 1.7. Using a word processor to record a decision a person has already made does. Whether 1.7 is met is a fact about the entity's systems. It cannot be settled by reading the policy. A policy with no disclosure may belong to an entity that is outside the trigger. Epworth is the one row that says so in terms.

**APP 1.8 is the text.** If 1.7 is met, the privacy policy must state three things:

- the kinds of personal information used in the operation of such computer programs;
- the kinds of decisions made solely by the operation of such computer programs;
- the kinds of decisions for which a thing, substantially and directly related to making the decision, is done by the operation of such computer programs.

Those three sentences are the only part of the obligation that shows up, or fails to show up, on a public page. "Compliant on its face" in this file means those three are present in the Australian section of the policy. It means the text is there. It does not mean the text matches what the software does.

**APP 1.9 is the definition of a decision, for the purposes of 1.7 and 1.8.** Making a decision includes refusing or failing to make one. Doing a thing includes refusing or failing to do it. The effect on the person may be adverse or beneficial. The OAIC's examples of a decision that may affect rights or interests are a decision under an Act to grant or refuse a benefit (a visa, a housing payment), a decision that affects rights under a contract (a life-insurance policy), and a decision that affects access to a significant service or support (healthcare). 1.9 widens what counts. It does not add a fourth paragraph the policy has to contain.

The tables therefore mention 1.8 because 1.8 is the disclosure. 1.7 is the reason the disclosure is required, and 1.9 is the reason a refusal, a failure to decide, and a beneficial decision are all in scope. A policy can attend to 1.7 and 1.9 and still fail this file's screen, if it never writes the three lists.

### Who has to comply, and why state and territory bodies do not

The APPs apply to an APP entity. An APP entity is an Australian Government agency, or an organisation the *Privacy Act 1988* covers (a business over the turnover threshold, and some smaller ones, including most health-service providers). Section 4 of the Act binds the Crown. Section 4(3) then says the Act does not make the Crown in right of a State, the Australian Capital Territory or the Northern Territory an agency. The OAIC states the consequence in its own words: the Privacy Act does not cover local, state or territory government agencies, other than the Norfolk Island administration.

A Queensland government department is the Crown in right of Queensland. It is not an agency under the Privacy Act, and APP 1.7–1.9 were never imposed on it. Its privacy statute is the *Information Privacy Act 2009* (Qld). The Queensland Privacy Principles are in schedule 3. QPP 1, in the Act current as at 29 October 2026, requires a privacy policy and lists what it must contain: the kinds of personal information, how they are collected and held, the purposes, access and correction, complaints, and overseas disclosure. The list stops there. The fetched text of the Act contains no computer-program disclosure and no equivalent of APP 1.7, 1.8 or 1.9.

Two limits on that sentence, so it is not read as "nothing in Queensland is covered":

- A Queensland company is a different person. Bank of Queensland is in this frame as an APP entity, and its policy was read against APP 1.8.
- A Queensland government-owned corporation is excluded from the Information Privacy Act and can be an organisation under the Privacy Act. A tax-file-number record, and a state body delivering a Commonwealth program under a contract that applies the APPs, can bring a slice of activity under the Commonwealth Act. The Queensland rows in this file are a whole-of-government landing page and a department. Both pages present the entity as bound by the Queensland Act.

The same split is why Service NSW, Service Victoria, icare, WorkSafe Victoria, the Western Australian transport department, the South Australian Department of Human Services, Service Tasmania, Access Canberra, Territory Services in the Northern Territory, and the ten public universities are marked **Outside the APPs**. The page was still read. Those rows are not non-compliance with APP 1.8.

⭐⭐ **And that is worth more than a footnote, because it is easy to read "outside the APPs" as "unprotected", and it is not what it means.** Every state and territory has built privacy protection for its own public sector — by different instruments, at different times, and with different coverage:

▸ **New South Wales** — the *Privacy and Personal Information Protection Act 1998*, with health information under the *Health Records and Information Privacy Act 2002*.
▸ **Victoria** — the *Privacy and Data Protection Act 2014*.
▸ **Queensland** — the *Information Privacy Act 2009*, whose privacy principles include a privacy-policy duty.
▸ **Tasmania** — the *Personal Information Protection Act 2004*.
▸ **Australian Capital Territory** — the *Information Privacy Act 2014*.
▸ **Northern Territory** — the *Information Act 2002*.
▸ ⭐⭐ **Western Australia** — the *Privacy and Responsible Information Sharing Act 2024*, assented 6 December 2024. **Schedule 1, which carries the Information Privacy Principles, came into operation on 1 July 2026**, and the WA Government's own page opens with *"New privacy and data sharing laws commence on 1 July 2026."* ⭐ **This is the one instrument in this list that addresses automated decisions directly, and the shape it takes is the part worth carrying away.** **IPP 10 applies where an IPP entity employs an automated decision-making process involving personal information in making significant decisions about individuals.** It reaches past fully automated systems: **s 16(2) also covers a decision whose making is *"materially assisted by an automated system"***, which s 16(3) defines as a decision made in reliance on a preliminary step having *"a material bearing on the decision that is made."* **A significant decision is one affecting an individual's *"rights, entitlements, interests or liabilities"*, or otherwise having a significant effect on their *"life circumstances, opportunities, behaviour or wellbeing"* (s 16(4)).** The standing duties are **an assessment of the process's impact**, addressing harm, bias and discrimination and whether a human-intervention route exists, **periodic evaluation of its operation and effectiveness**, and **reassessment whenever the process changes** (IPP 10.1). Then, for each significant decision: **notice to the individual that an automated process was employed**, **information on request about how it is employed**, and **a process by which the individual can request human intervention** (IPP 10.2) — information that must be *"reasonably comprehensive"* and in a form understandable *"by a person without specialist knowledge"* (IPP 10.4). ⚠️ **IPP 10 applies only to personal information collected on or after the commencement day (s 223(2)(d)).**

▸ **South Australia** — the **Information Privacy Principles Instruction**, issued as **Premier and Cabinet Circular PC012**. ⭐ **It binds South Australian public sector agencies by administrative direction rather than by statute**, having begun in 1989 as a Cabinet administrative instruction, and State Records South Australia describes it as the principal privacy instrument for State Government agencies.


⭐ **So the ten universities and the state service agencies in this frame are governed, just governed by somebody else.** A public university established under state legislation answers to its state's regime for its public functions.

⭐⭐ **Western Australia is the instructive comparison, because the same transparency goal takes a different shape there.** **APP 1.8 asks for words in a published privacy policy.** **WA's IPP 10 attaches to the decision itself** — notice to the person affected, an explanation on request, and a route to a human. **WA's privacy-policy duty is separate, and lighter.** **IPP 5.1 requires an IPP entity to develop a document setting out its policies on handling personal information and to make it available to anyone who requests it**, and the automated-decision item sits in **IPP 5.3(d)**, which requires the entity, *on request*, to let a person know generally *"whether any personal information held by the IPP entity is used for an automated decision-making process."* ⭐ **One item, on request — rather than three disclosures, published in advance.**

⛔ **What matters for this survey is narrower and it is the only claim made here: of the state and territory instruments consulted, the three APP 1.8 disclosures do not appear.** The Commonwealth has legislated a specific transparency duty about automated decisions. **The equivalent duty was not found in the state and territory principles read for this survey** — which is a statement about what was read, not a census of Australian privacy law.

⭐⭐ **The practical consequence for a technologist is the one worth carrying away.** An organisation can sit outside APP 1.7–1.9 and still be running exactly the same scoring, eligibility and triage systems as the organisation next to it that is inside. **The systems do not change at the border; only the disclosure duty does.**

## 2. Method — so this can be repeated and challenged

> ⛔⭐⭐ **This is a coarse screen, and the regulator's own test is finer.** It is worth saying so before the method, because the difference decides what these tables can and cannot be used for.
>
> **This survey asks a text question:** are the three disclosures present in the published privacy policy? **The OAIC asks a sufficiency question:** is the level of detail *"meaningful to a reasonable person"*? ⭐ **Its supplementary fact sheet for government agencies works the difference through with an example** — an agency using AI for eligibility decisions on government support payments might classify that under a usage pattern and give a brief overview, and the OAIC says **that level of detail would not be sufficient**, because the agency may use several programs for those decisions; it would expect the policy to expand on it and name the provision of government support payments.
>
> ⚠️ **So a row marked ✅ here means the three disclosures are present, not that they are adequate.** Telstra's two sentences pass this screen; whether *"administration"* is meaningful to a reasonable person is the regulator's question and not one this file answers. ⭐ **A coarse screen is still useful — it is reproducible, it is checkable row by row, and a policy that lacks the lists entirely fails the fine test as well.** But the fine test is the OAIC's, and the fact sheet is the document to read next.

1. **Retrieve the live policy** at its canonical URL. If that URL failed, a second URL was tried (the link the first page pointed at, a PDF, or a search result that returned the policy text).
2. **Search the full text** for `automated decision`, `solely or substantially`, `substantially and directly`, `kinds of decisions`, `computer program`, and the neighbouring phrases `artificial intelligence`, `automated processing` and `profiling`.
3. **Classify** against the three disclosures APP 1.8 requires:
   - the **kinds of personal information** used by the programs;
   - the **kinds of decisions** made **solely** by a computer program;
   - the **kinds of decisions** a program does something **substantially and directly related** to making.

### Classification used

| Status | Means |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Compliant on its face** | All three disclosures present, in the Australian section of the policy. Telstra's combined "solely or substantially" list is treated as covering both decision limbs |
| ◐ **Partial** | The Australian policy has a passage about automated or AI decision-making, scoring, filtering, recommendations or eligibility, and at least one of the three disclosures is absent |
| ⚠️ **GDPR-shaped only** | An automated-decision section exists but is framed on GDPR Article 22 (right to object, right to human review) and sits in a UK/EEA section — **it does not satisfy APP 1.8, and the two are easy to confuse** |
| ❌ **No disclosure found** | No APP 1.8 disclosure. A bare statement that AI tools are used, with no decision described, is recorded in the evidence and still classified here |
| **Declares not engaged** | The policy says the organisation does not make decisions that significantly affect rights or interests by automated means, and says it will update the policy if that changes |
| **Outside the APPs** | The governing instrument is a state privacy Act. The published page was still read. These rows are not non-compliance with APP 1.8 |
| **Not retrieved** | The policy body was not obtained. The row lists what was tried |

⚠️ **"Compliant on its face" means the text is present, not that it is accurate or complete.** Whether a policy actually describes what an organisation's software does is not checkable from outside, and this survey does not attempt it.

⚠️ **Policies change without notice and some carry no effective date.** Every row is an observation on 12 November 2026 only. Where the page stated a date, it is in the evidence. Where it did not, the evidence says so.

## 5. The one worked example — Telstra

**Telstra Privacy Statement, in effect 1 July 2026.** Section headed **"Automated decisions"**, quoted in full:

> *"We sometimes use automated decision-making to support us with our business functions and activities. The kinds of personal information used when making solely or substantially automated decisions include identity information, financial information, concession details, information to protect against fraud, crime or misconduct, activity on our website, systems and applications, information about products and services you have with us, and information about your use of our products and services.*
>
> *The kinds of solely or substantially automated decisions that we may arrange to occur include decisions relating to providing products and services to you, verifying your identity and creditworthiness, administration, network and security monitoring, and direct marketing."*

### Why this example is worth more than its compliance

⭐⭐ **Every category it names is already a heading elsewhere in the same document.** The information categories — identity, financial, concession details, fraud/crime/misconduct, website activity — are verbatim the subheadings of its own "What personal information do we collect?" section. The decision categories — providing products and services, verifying identity, administration, network and security monitoring, direct marketing — are verbatim the subheadings of its "How do we use your personal information?" section.

⭐⭐ **The whole disclosure is a cross-reference to material the policy already contained.** It is two sentences and under 120 words. **That is what enumerate-then-group produces**, and it is the strongest available demonstration that the obligation is a description exercise rather than a drafting one.

⭐ **"That we may arrange to occur"** tracks the OAIC's "arranged for" reading, which indicates drafting against the guidance rather than paraphrase of it.

⚠️ **It is minimal compliance, not a model.** *"Administration"* is about as broad as a category can be while remaining a category. **A reader cannot tell from this disclosure what the software actually does** — which is a fair observation about the obligation's ceiling, and a limit on how much any policy text can be expected to deliver.

⭐ **Rio Tinto's external statement, effective 6 October 2026, is the second text in this frame that meets the same bar**, and it is more specific in one place: the in-vehicle safety example names the data (fatigue, eye movement, driver behaviour, location) and the decision (whether the person can continue driving). It still combines the two decision limbs in one sentence, and it sends recruitment to another statement.

## 8. Why so few of these policies carry the three lists

The count in §1 is two policies, out of 120, with all three APP 1.8 disclosures on 12 November 2026. That count is not a compliance rate. Read against the Act and the dates on the policies, it has a plain explanation, and the explanation is only partly the one that there is no consequence.

**The obligation is not in force.** Commencement is 10 December 2026. On the survey date there is no breach, no infringement notice, and no civil penalty available for a policy that lacks the three lists. An organisation that waits until the week of commencement is inside the law as it stands today. Telstra's statement has been in effect since 1 July 2026, and Rio Tinto's external statement since 6 October 2026, so waiting is a choice some large organisations have already declined. It is still a lawful choice for the other 28 days.

**When the obligation is in force, the consequence for a missing paragraph is real and it is small.** APP 1.8 is content that APP 1.4 requires in the privacy policy. Section 13K of the Privacy Act makes a breach of APP 1.4 a civil penalty provision for which an infringement notice can be issued. Section 13K(4) says the penalty must not exceed 200 penalty units. The OAIC's infringement-notice page, published 22 September 2026, uses a penalty unit of \$364 as at July 2026, which makes 200 units \$72,800. The amount stated in an infringement notice for a single contravention is 60 penalty units (\$21,840) for a body corporate, and 200 penalty units (\$72,800) where the body is a listed corporation. That is the tier Parliament put privacy-policy content into, in the same list as a missing direct-marketing opt-out. The serious-or-repeated interference penalties sit in other sections. The note to s 13K records that the same conduct may also contravene those sections. A missing paragraph, on its own, is the infringement-notice provision.

The infringement-notice power applies to conduct after 11 December 2024. APP 1.7–1.9 do not apply until 10 December 2026. No notice can have been issued for this disclosure, because the disclosure is not yet required.

**"There is no consequence, so nobody wastes the time" is therefore exact for the period before commencement, and too strong for the period after it.** After 10 December there is a consequence. It is an OAIC investigation, a possible determination, and a low-tier penalty of the size above. There is no right to sue the organisation for the missing paragraph. Set against the cost of a lawyer reading every scoring, eligibility and fraud system and signing a public list, a future notice of about \$22,000 for an ordinary company, or about \$73,000 for a listed company, is a weak reason to move five weeks early. It is also a weak reason to treat the words as optional once they are required. The dates in this file show both halves. Policies revised after the OAIC's guidance of 30 September 2026 that still lack the three lists include Foxtel (effective 1 November 2026), Youi (29 October 2026), Australia Post (20 October 2026) and Services Australia (stamps of 26 August 2026 and 15 October 2026). Rio's statement, effective 6 October 2026, has the lists, in a short passage. The work of writing them is small once the systems are known. The work of deciding one is in scope is the part organisations are leaving until the due date.

**A large share of the frame has written something adjacent and missed the three lists.** Twenty-six policies have a passage about automated or AI decisions, scoring, filtering or eligibility, and are partial because at least one of the three disclosures is absent. Youi's table of six processes, updated 29 October 2026, is the fullest of those. Comcare, last reviewed August 2026, uses the phrase "substantially and directly related" and lists kinds of personal information and kinds of fraud and corruption decisions, and still does not list a decision made solely by a program. Two more, Commonwealth Bank and nib, have automated-decision sections written for GDPR Article 22 and placed in a UK or EEA part of the policy. That wording is easy to mistake for the Australian requirement, and it does not meet it.

**Silence is also what APP 1.7 produces when the trigger is not met.** A missing paragraph is not proof the organisation arranges for a program to make a significant decision. Epworth says the precondition is not met and that the policy will be updated if that changes. Others may have reached the same conclusion and left the policy blank. This file cannot tell those two silences apart.

**Twenty-one rows are outside the APPs.** They include every state and territory body in the frame. They are not evidence that an APP entity has ignored the duty.

**The guidance is six weeks old.** The OAIC published the fact sheet, the flowchart, the government supplementary fact sheet and the revised APP 1 chapter on 30 September 2026. A large organisation's policy cycle is often longer than six weeks. That explains a policy last touched in 2024 or in July 2023, of which DEWR's is an example. It does not explain a policy opened in October or November 2026, after the guidance, that discusses automated decisions or AI and still omits the three lists. Several of those exist in this frame.

The hypothesis to carry forward is this. Before 10 December 2026 there is nothing to enforce, and the enforcement that arrives on that date is a low-tier privacy-policy penalty, not a ban, not a duty to tell the person at the time of the decision, and not a right of human review. Organisations that have already opened their policies are still, in the majority of dated cases, leaving the three lists out. That is consistent with a calculation that the words can wait until the week they become law. It is a poor description of Telstra and Rio, who published early, and it will be testable on 11 December, when the same pages can be read again and an absence will for the first time be a breach if APP 1.7 applies.

## 9. What Commonwealth agencies have published, and what they have not

The agencies that decide payments, tax, visas, disability, veterans' entitlements, passports and aged-care regulation are the entities for which APP 1.9's examples were written. A reader looking for evidence that those agencies are implementing the disclosure finds a different set of documents, and one privacy policy that comes close.

**The Royal Commission asked for a legal framework, and the framework is not law.** The report of the Royal Commission into the Robodebt Scheme recommended that the Commonwealth consider a consistent legal framework for automation in government services. The OAIC's report of 21 January 2026 quotes recommendation 17.1: a clear path to review; departmental websites that say automated decision-making is used and explain in plain language how the process works; business rules and algorithms available for independent expert scrutiny. On 1 April 2026 a minister told the House that the government is developing a whole-of-government framework for automated decision-making, implementing the Royal Commission's recommendations. That is a statement that work is underway. On 7 September 2026 Kate Chaney MP introduced a private member's bill, the Automated Decision-Making (Safeguards and Transparency) Bill 2026, and moved the second reading the same day. The bill would require a person to be told when a decision affecting them was automated, and would establish a public register. A parliamentary tracker consulted for this version still has the bill before the House at the second-reading stage. It is not a government bill, it is not enacted, and it is a different instrument from APP 1.8. APP 1.8 requires three lists in a privacy policy. It does not require the person to be told at the time of the decision, and it does not require the algorithm to be published.

**The OAIC has already measured a prior transparency duty and found it thin.** In October 2025 the OAIC reviewed the websites of 23 Commonwealth agencies that have a statutory authorisation to use automated decision-making, and published the report on 21 January 2026. Four of the 23 disclosed in their Information Publication Scheme material that they use automated decision-making. The agencies that did disclose were not clear about how. The review found no agency with a published guideline or policy on automated decision-making. That review is about the Freedom of Information Act, not about APP 1.8, and it predates the September 2026 guidance. It is evidence that, ten months before this survey, agencies already authorised to automate were mostly silent in the place an existing Act required operational information to be published.

**The document agencies have been writing is the AI transparency statement, and the regulator has said it is the wrong document for this duty.** Under the Digital Transformation Agency's Policy for the responsible use of AI in government, in-scope agencies publish an AI transparency statement. The DTA's list of those statements was last updated on 23 June 2026, and the DTA does not check their contents. The OAIC's supplementary fact sheet for government agencies, published with the 30 September 2026 guidance, says the two documents answer different questions. An AI statement can classify a use as "decision-making and administrative action" and give a brief overview. The same fact sheet says that level of detail fails APP 1.8, and its example is eligibility for government support payments: the privacy policy has to identify the decisions and the personal information. The fact sheet also says a computer program that is not AI does not have to appear in the AI statement at all. A rule-based calculator of the kind used in income compliance is in that second class. Building the AI statement does not produce the APP 1.8 lists, and it does not cover the non-AI programs.

**The privacy policies in this frame match that split.** Services Australia's policy, touched on 26 August 2026 and stamped again on 15 October 2026, has one sentence and points to an Automation and AI Transparency Statement last updated 10 October 2025. The statement describes governance and a human in the loop. It does not set out the three lists. DVA names the sections that authorise a computer to decide (the Veterans' Entitlements Act, the Defence-related Claims Act, and the Military Rehabilitation and Compensation Act) and does not list the kinds of personal information or the two limbs. The ATO policy was updated on 3 September 2026, a month before the guidance, and has none of the phrases. The NDIA page, last updated September 2026, has none. Australia Post, 20 October 2026, has none. Home Affairs' policy body was not retrieved; the overview of 31 July 2026 describes uses of AI and points to an AI transparency statement. DFAT, updated September 2026, says an officer remains responsible for decisions AI tools support, names Copilot, and points to its AI statement. The Department of Education, August 2026, and the Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission, August 2026, mention AI tools and do not describe a decision. DEWR's policy was last updated in July 2023. The OAIC's own privacy policy was updated on 22 October 2025, has not been revised since the office published the guidance, and does not contain the three lists.

**Comcare is the exception, and it is partial.** Its privacy policy, last reviewed August 2026, says ADM systems will be substantially and directly related to decisions on suspected fraud and corruption, gives examples (further review, referral for compliance action, criminal investigation), lists the kinds of personal information, and says a person oversees the decision. It does not list a decision made solely by a program. It was reviewed before the OAIC fact sheet. It shows that an agency can write against the words of the Act without a whole-of-government template. It also shows the screen this file uses: the statutory phrase and a human-oversight sentence are not yet the three lists.

**A fine is a weak explanation for an agency.** The Act binds the Commonwealth. Section 4(2) says it does not render the Crown liable to be prosecuted for an offence, which is a criminal-prosecution rule. The remedy the OAIC actually uses against an agency is an investigation and a determination requiring the practice to change. An infringement notice, if one were issued to a department, would move money inside the Commonwealth. That is a poor account of why a privacy policy is unchanged. The better account, on the documents, is that the effort since Robodebt has gone into an AI-statement regime, an FOI publication duty the OAIC has already found mostly unmet, a framework the government said in April 2026 it was developing, and a private member's bill that is not law. APP 1.8 asks for something narrower than any of those, three lists in the privacy policy, and the payment, tax, disability and veterans policies do not yet contain them. The duty is weeks from commencement. **Absence today is not a breach of anything.** ⚠️ **Whether absence on 11 December would amount to one, in a policy that still points at an AI statement and does not set out the lists, is a question for the regulator and not for me** — and the purpose of taking this snapshot before the date is to let somebody ask it with the evidence already gathered.

⭐ **The conventional next step** is to read this frame again on 11 December 2026 and treat a still-missing list, where APP 1.7 applies, as a breach of a duty that has started.

⭐ **The other step, which the documents support now,** is to score each agency's AI transparency statement against the three APP 1.8 lists in public, before commencement, and to say in terms where the statement fails. The OAIC has already written the test, using eligibility for a support payment as the example. Doing that scoring uses the before-snapshot this file has just taken. The risk is that agencies answer the scoring exercise and leave the privacy policy for the week of 10 December, so the artefact that moves is the statement and the legal duty sits in the policy. The payoff is that the gap stops being a private drafting problem and becomes a list a minister can be asked about while there are still 28 days.

## About this file, and its limits

**I am not a lawyer or a legal practitioner, and nothing in this file is legal advice.** It is a record of my own reading of published web pages, written for people who build and run software and who want to know what a new disclosure duty looks like in practice.

⛔ **It is not a compliance assessment of any organisation.** Whether an organisation is subject to APP 1.7 at all depends on facts about its own systems — whether a computer program makes or substantially and directly contributes to a decision, whether personal information is used, and whether the effect on rights or interests is significant. ⭐ **None of those facts can be established by reading a privacy policy**, which is why this file scores the text of the policy and nothing else, and why a missing disclosure may simply mean the trigger is not met. One organisation in the frame says exactly that in terms.

⛔ **The obligation does not commence until 10 December 2026.** **Nothing recorded here could be a contravention, because at the time of the survey there was no duty to contravene.** Any reading of these tables as a league table of lawbreakers is a misreading, and an organisation is free to update its policy the day before commencement and be entirely in order.

⚠️ **The classifications are mine, and another careful reader would draw some of them differently.** An independent survey published on 23 September 2026 used a looser screen and scored two organisations complete that this file records as partial. **That disagreement is recorded rather than resolved**, because the two screens are not the same screen.

⚠️ **The data decays immediately.** Policies change without notice, often without a version or a date. **Every row is an observation about one URL on one day**, and the day is given on the row.

⚠️ **Where a count appears, it is a count of the named organisations in this frame.** ⛔ **It is not a rate, a proportion, a sample, or an estimate of anything about Australian organisations generally.** The frame is 120 organisations I chose, and the choosing was mine.

## Where this leads

⭐⭐ **The interesting finding in this file is not the count. It is why the count is what it is.**

**Writing the three lists is small work.** Rio Tinto's version is a short passage. Telstra's is two sentences assembled from headings already in its own policy. ⭐ **Neither is a drafting achievement — both are organisations that knew what their software decides.**

⛔ **The hard part is knowing.** A policy author cannot describe the kinds of decisions a program makes unless somebody can enumerate them, and an enumeration is only reliable if it comes from a record rather than from memory and a meeting. **That is the gap these 120 policies are evidence of**, four weeks out from a duty that has been public for two years.

⭐ **So the useful response is not a drafting exercise. It is a recording one.** Three things, written at the time a decision is made and not reconstructed afterwards:

▸ **What was decided**, and against what standard set in advance.
▸ **Why it went that way** — one short answer from a fixed, predefined list.
▸ **What it rested on** — the identifiers of what was actually in front of the decider.

⭐⭐ **An organisation that has those three can answer the 10 December question by running a query.** It can also answer the questions that follow it: which decisions relied on a number that turned out to be wrong, and why this decision went this way for this person. **The disclosure duty is the first question of that kind to arrive with a date attached. It will not be the last.**
